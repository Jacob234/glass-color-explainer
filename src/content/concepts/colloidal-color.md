Colloidal color in glass comes from metal nanoparticles (gold, copper, or silver) that are grown inside the glass after it is formed and then absorb light. The mechanism is physically distinct from dissolved-ion color: here the absorbing entity is a metallic particle tens of nanometers across, not a single ion. The phenomenon is called **surface plasmon resonance**, and it produces stronger colors than dissolved ions can: the red of gold ruby, the crimson of copper ruby, the yellow of [silver stain](../silver-stain/).

A defining feature of the mechanism is that **the glass has no color when it leaves the furnace**. A gold ruby gather fresh from the melt is colorless or faintly tinted. The color appears only during a controlled reheat called striking, which nucleates and grows the colloids. A goblet unstruck in storage looks like clear glass; held over a torch for the right amount of time, it turns red. Because the color appears late and has to be judged during the reheat, ruby glass was one of the most valued and difficult products of medieval and Baroque glassmaking.

## How it works

When a metal nanoparticle much smaller than the wavelength of light sits inside a transparent glass, an incoming light wave drives the particle's conduction electrons into collective oscillation. At the resonance frequency, the particle absorbs light far more strongly per atom than any dissolved ion. For gold spheres roughly 5–60 nm across in silicate glass, the resonance sits near 530 nm (green): green is absorbed, and the transmitted light is the ruby red for which gold glass has been known for two thousand years.

**Particle size tunes the color.** As gold particles grow coarser during striking, the resonance shifts and broadens. A correctly struck ruby is clear red; over-striking pushes it toward purple, then a muddy blue-gray, which glassmakers call *livered* glass. The defect is an error in particle size at the nanometer scale that is visible to the eye.

Silver resonates at shorter wavelengths, near 410–450 nm (blue absorbed, yellow transmitted), which is why silver in glass gives yellow rather than red. Copper's resonance sits near 565 nm with additional interband absorption, producing an oxblood red similar to gold's at far lower cost.

**Striking.** The metal enters the melt in ionic form — gold dissolved via aqua regia, copper as an oxide — accompanied by a reducing agent. Historically, tin was the usual reductant for gold, and the standard gold preparation for ruby glass was **Purple of Cassius**, a gold-tin co-precipitate described by Andreas Cassius in the 1680s. The Potsdam glassmaker **Johann Kunckel** turned this chemistry into reliable ruby glass production in the same decade, after decades of experimentation in European workshops.

On the reheat (typically 500–700 °C), reduced metal atoms nucleate and aggregate into colloids. Time and temperature control the particle size, and particle size controls the color, so the striking schedule is part of the recipe.

## Artifact stories

### Gold ruby

Gold ruby glass contains actual gold — roughly 0.01–0.1% by weight — and this cost means it is often used as **flashed glass** for sheet and window applications, a thin ruby layer fused over a clear gather. Solid body color is used for vessels and tableware, where the thin walls keep gold content manageable. In the dilute form known as **cranberry glass**, the gold loading is lower still, producing pink rather than red. Cranberry became a popular Victorian tableware color: the pink shows well in thin-blown pieces, and the dilution kept costs down.

Ruby glass dates back to ancient Rome, but Kunckel's 17th-century Potsdam workshop established the chemical principles that made production reliable. The ware he made — now held in museum collections across Europe — shows the full range from pale cranberry pink to deep garnet, the variation arising from different striking schedules on the same base composition.

Copper ruby follows the same striking logic and is the industrial and historical substitute when gold is too expensive. Medieval reds in [stained glass](../stained-glass/) windows are almost always copper ruby, flashed thin over clear glass because a full-thickness copper ruby sheet looks nearly black.

### The Lycurgus Cup

The Lycurgus Cup, a Roman cage cup now in the British Museum dated to approximately the 4th century CE, is the best-known example of nanoparticle color in glass. The cup contains roughly 70 nm gold-silver alloy particles that both absorb and scatter light.

In reflected light (under normal room lighting) it appears opaque pea-green, because scattered light dominates. Lit from behind, it is translucent wine-red, because the eye receives light transmitted through the absorbing colloid. No pigment is involved; both colors come from the nanoparticles, and the craftsmen who made it did not know their colorant was metallic particles. The composition was not identified until electron microscopy analysis in the 1990s.

The Lycurgus Cup is the standard example of ancient, unintended nanotechnology. It shows that empirical craft knowledge can control structure at the nanoscale centuries before there is scientific language to describe it.
