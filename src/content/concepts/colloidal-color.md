Colloidal color in glass works by growing metal nanoparticles — gold, copper, or silver — inside the glass after it is formed, then letting those particles absorb light. The mechanism is physically distinct from dissolved-ion color: here the absorbing entity is a metallic particle tens of nanometers across, not a single ion. The phenomenon is called **surface plasmon resonance**, and it produces colors of an intensity and depth that dissolved ions cannot match: the deep red of gold ruby, the warm crimson of copper ruby, the yellow of [silver stain](../silver-stain/).

The mechanism's signature feature is that **the color does not exist when the glass leaves the furnace**. A gold ruby gather fresh from the melt is colorless or faintly tinted. The color appears only during a controlled reheat called striking, which nucleates and grows the colloids. A goblet unstruck in storage looks like clear glass; held over a torch for the right amount of time, it blooms red. This delayed appearance — and the skill required to read it — made gold ruby one of the most prized and difficult products of the Baroque glassmaker's repertoire.

## How it works

When a metal nanoparticle much smaller than the wavelength of light sits inside a transparent glass, the particle's conduction electrons behave like a cloud in a box: an incoming light wave drives them into collective oscillation. At the resonance frequency, the particle absorbs light ferociously — far more strongly per atom than any dissolved ion. For gold spheres a few to a few tens of nanometres across in silicate glass, the resonance sits near 530 nm (green; the size range is unverified): green is absorbed, and the transmitted remainder is the ruby red of gold glass.

**Particle size tunes the color.** As gold particles grow coarser, the resonance shifts toward longer wavelengths and broadens (the direction Mie theory gives for larger spheres). A well-struck ruby is clear red. What an over-struck glass looks like is less settled than glassmaking lore suggests: in Haslbeck and colleagues' gold ruby glasses, fewer and larger particles gave purple-red while tin-rich glasses with many small particles went brown. This site's [reheat stage](../../#strike) models over-striking as the band moving about 30 nm red and broadening, which computes to a dull purple; the "livery" brown of the workshop is not modelled, and we have found no measured spectrum of over-struck gold ruby to check either against.

Silver resonates at shorter wavelengths, near 410–450 nm (unverified range) (blue absorbed, yellow transmitted), which is why silver in glass gives yellow rather than red. Copper's resonance sits near 565 nm (unverified) with additional interband absorption, producing a deep oxblood red similar to gold's but achievable at far lower cost.

**Striking.** The metal enters the melt in ionic form — gold dissolved via aqua regia, copper as an oxide — accompanied by a reducing agent. Historically, tin was the classic companion of gold, and the standard gold preparation for ruby glass was **Purple of Cassius**, a gold-tin precipitate named for Andreas Cassius of Hamburg, whose *De Auro* appeared in 1685. The Corning Museum credits a similar preparation to Johann Rudolf Glauber earlier still. **Johann Kunckel** had perfected gold ruby at Potsdam shortly before 1679, and by the mid-1680s was making it at the Elector of Brandenburg's glassworks there with purple of Cassius in the batch (Corning Museum of Glass).

On the reheat — around 600 °C in Haslbeck and colleagues' study, and in general a few hundred degrees below the melt — reduced metal atoms nucleate and aggregate into colloids. Time and temperature control the particle size, and particle size controls the color. The striking schedule *is* the recipe.

## Artifact stories

### Gold ruby

Gold ruby glass contains actual gold, though very little: the laboratory gold rubies of Haslbeck and colleagues hold 200 parts per million (0.02% by weight), and the Lycurgus Cup's glass as a whole about 40 ppm. Even so, the cost means it is often used as **flashed glass** for sheet and window applications, a thin ruby layer fused over a clear gather. Solid body color is used for vessels and tableware, where the thin walls keep gold content manageable. In the dilute form known as **cranberry glass**, the gold loading is lower still, producing a warm pink rather than a deep red. Cranberry became a popular Victorian tableware color (unverified): the delicate pink reads jewel-like in thin-blown pieces, and the dilution kept costs manageable.

Roman glassmakers did get gold into glass, as the Lycurgus Cup below shows, but only a handful of other Roman dichroic pieces are known, and gold ruby made as a matter of routine starts in 17th-century Europe, usually credited to Kunckel (Freestone and colleagues, 2007). Potsdam ruby vessels date from about 1685 to 1700 and range from pale pink to deep garnet; how much of that range comes from striking rather than gold content is not established here.

Copper ruby follows the same striking logic and is the industrial and historical substitute when gold is too expensive. Medieval reds in [stained glass](../stained-glass/) windows are almost always copper ruby, flashed thin over clear glass because a full-thickness copper ruby sheet reads nearly black (general account, unverified here).

### The Lycurgus Cup

The Lycurgus Cup, a Roman cage cup now in the British Museum dated to approximately the 4th century CE, is the canonical demonstration of what nanoparticles in glass can do. Its glass holds about 300 ppm of silver and 40 ppm of gold, much of it as silver-gold alloy particles 50–100 nm across (roughly 7 parts silver to 3 of gold, with some copper) that both absorb and scatter light (Freestone and colleagues, 2007).

In reflected light — looking at the cup under normal room lighting — it appears opaque pea-green: scattered light dominates. Lit from behind, it glows translucent wine-red: transmitted light passing through the absorbing colloid reaches the eye. One object, two colors, zero pigment. The effect is pure nanoparticle optics, and the craftsmen who made it had no idea their colorant was metallic particles at all. Robert Brill's analyses in the 1960s tied the color to the traces of gold and silver (R. H. Brill, "The chemistry of the Lycurgus Cup", VII International Congress on Glass, Brussels, 1965, paper 223, as summarised by Freestone and colleagues); D. J. Barber and I. C. Freestone confirmed in 1990, by analytical transmission electron microscopy, that the metals sit as alloy nanoparticles.

The Lycurgus Cup is the standard exhibit-A for ancient accidental nanotechnology, and it demonstrates something worth holding: people can achieve nanoscale precision through empirical craft knowledge centuries before they have the scientific language to describe what they are doing.
