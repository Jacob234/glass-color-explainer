Stained glass is the architectural art of colored-glass windows. In terms of glass chemistry, it used every mechanism of color centuries before any of them was understood. A Gothic window combines ionic cobalt blues from [transition metal colorants](../transition-metal-colorants/), flashed colloidal copper reds from [colloidal color](../colloidal-color/), silver-nanoparticle yellows from [silver stain](../silver-stain/), and fired iron-oxide paint, assembled into pictures with strips of lead. The medium reached an artistic peak in the 12th–13th centuries that, by common judgment, has never been surpassed.

The name is partly a misnomer: most of the color is not stain but **pot-metal**, glass colored throughout its body in the melt. The one true stain, silver stain (from around 1310), was influential enough to name the whole medium.

## The glazier's toolkit

**Pot-metal glass** is the basic material: glass colored in the melt at the furnace. Cobalt blue, manganese purple, iron and chromium greens, copper turquoise: each is a dissolved-ion colorant in the silicate melt, which is cast or blown into sheet. Every color is a separate sheet of glass; the design is mosaicked from pieces. The redox conditions in the furnace ([redox in the furnace](../redox/)) help determine the color: pot copper fired under oxidizing conditions gives a different color than pot copper under reducing ones.

**Flashed glass** is the solution to red. Copper ruby glass — the colloidal-color mechanism, red from copper nanoparticles — is so optically dense at full thickness that a pane of it reads nearly black. The medieval answer was flashing: dip the gather of clear glass into a thin coating of ruby, so the final sheet is a micron-scale ruby layer over a clear body. Flashed glass also allows a design technique: the colored layer can be selectively abraded away to expose the clear layer underneath, allowing two-tone effects in a single piece. Heraldic windows used this for coats of arms, and the technique remains in use.

**Vitreous paint (grisaille)** adds painted detail over the mosaic color. Iron oxide or copper oxide pigments, ground with powdered glass flux, are painted onto the finished pieces for faces, drapery, inscriptions, and detail. The painted pane is then kiln-fired so the pigment melts into the glass surface, fusing permanently. The result survives weathering that would strip any organic paint in decades. Grisaille windows (gray-scale painted patterns on clear or white glass) use this technique as their only source of color; the 13th-century Cistercian tradition preferred it to colored glass on theological grounds.

**Silver stain** is the 14th-century innovation: a fired silver compound that grows yellow silver nanoparticles inside the glass surface (the mechanism is described in detail on [that page](../silver-stain/)). Unlike the other techniques here, it can be applied to part of a pane rather than the whole thing. This gives two colors, white and yellow, on one piece of glass with no lead line between them: yellow hair on a white face, a gold halo, a yellow border on a white robe. Window design changed visibly within a generation of the technique's arrival.

**Lead cames** are the structural system: H-section lead strips holding the glass pieces together, soldered at joints, the whole panel stiffened by iron armatures set into the masonry. The black lines are both structural and compositional; stained-glass designers have used the lead network as part of the drawing.

## Light as theology

The medieval rise of stained glass had an explicit intellectual program. Abbot Suger's rebuilding of the Abbey of Saint-Denis in the 1140s framed colored light (*lux nova*, new light) as a vehicle of the divine. The progressive replacement of stone wall by colored glass in Gothic buildings can be read as theology expressed in architecture: the building admits a heavenly light understood to transform, not merely illuminate. The structural innovations of Gothic architecture (pointed arches, rib vaults, flying buttresses) served, among other things, to move load away from walls so that the walls could become windows.

Chartres Cathedral, with its 12th- and 13th-century glass largely intact, remains the best single place to see what this program produced. The cobalt blue of its earliest windows, the *bleu de Chartres*, has survived eight centuries, including two world wars during which the glass was removed and reinstalled for protection. The Sainte-Chapelle in Paris goes further: the building is functionally a stone frame for 600 square meters of glass, with very little solid wall.

## The American turn

The late 19th century produced a second great stained-glass moment, this time in the United States, and it took the tradition in a different direction. **Louis Comfort Tiffany** and **John La Farge** — working independently in the 1870s and 1880s, competing bitterly over priority — developed **opalescent glass** for windows: glass with color, texture, and tonal gradation built into the body itself through structural scattering and colorant layering. Tiffany's studios produced thousands of windows and lamp shades whose imagery was carried entirely by the glass's own material properties, without relying on painted grisaille for modeling.

Where the Gothic tradition worked with glassblowers who made flat sheet glass and painters who worked on top of it, Tiffany's approach put the artistic content in the glass body, which reversed the medieval hierarchy and shifted the artistry to the glassmaker. La Farge is often credited with pioneering the use of copper foil as an alternative to heavy lead came for smaller, more intricate work; Tiffany industrialized and branded it. The resulting style, nature-themed and densely colored, became one of the most recognizable visual idioms of the American Gilded Age.

The Gothic and the Tiffany traditions remain the two poles of the medium: one built around geometry, theology, and the mosaicked plane; the other around naturalism and the material properties of the glass itself.
